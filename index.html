Scheduling exams without clashes, using randomized algorithms and a dash of evolution.

Hundreds of courses, thousands of students, a handful of slots — and not one student may sit two exams at once. This project turns that puzzle into a working scheduler built on Miller–Rabin primality testing, randomized QuickSort, QuickSelect, graph coloring, and a genetic algorithm.

How the scheduler thinks

Four stages, three of them randomized — exactly the Unit V toolkit, each doing real work.

1

Store conflicts fast

Hash tables sized to primes — verified by Miller–Rabin random witnesses — give O(1) “do these courses clash?” lookups.

2

Prioritize courses

Randomized QuickSort orders courses by conflict-degree, so the most constrained get placed first.

3

Pick the worst few

QuickSelect finds the K most conflict-heavy courses in expected O(n) — no full sort needed.

4

Let it evolve

A genetic algorithm (or simulated annealing) repairs the timetable until no edge shares a color.

Why this is hard

Model the semester as a graph: every course is a vertex; draw an edge between two courses whenever at least one student is enrolled in both. Assigning exam slots is now exactly graph coloring — adjacent vertices must get different colors. With a few hundred courses the number of possible timetables dwarfs the number of atoms in the universe.

NP-Hard. No known algorithm guarantees the perfect coloring quickly, so practical schedulers trade certainty for speed: smart heuristics plus randomized algorithms that are right with overwhelming probability and fast on average.

What the numbers say

On the default dataset the finished pipeline reaches a zero-conflict timetable in ~300 ms. The randomized pieces earn their keep measurably: on pre-sorted input a textbook QuickSort balloons to ~4× the comparisons while the randomized pivot barely notices, and QuickSelect answers “top 10 most conflict-heavy” with a third of a full sort's work.
